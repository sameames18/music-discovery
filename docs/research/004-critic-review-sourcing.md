# Critic Review sourcing per Publication (ticket 004)

Research for [ticket 004](../../wayfinder/tickets/004-research-critic-review-sourcing.md). Vocabulary per [CONTEXT.md](../../CONTEXT.md): a **Publication** is a named source of **Critic Reviews**; the **Critic Score** is our own aggregate. Selecting the Publication list is ticket 008; the normalisation formula is ticket 009. This document supplies facts for both.

**This is not legal advice.** The legal section reports what courts and primary sources say and flags what is unsettled.

## 1. Summary

- **Feeds are nearly universal; scores in feeds are not.** 30 of 36 Publications probed expose a working RSS/Atom feed. Only two carry the score inside the feed itself: **The Needle Drop** (score as an RSS `<category>`, e.g. `8/10`) and **The Guardian** (via its Content API, not RSS — `fields.starRating`). Every other Publication needs the review page fetched to get the score.
- **Machine-readable scores on review pages (schema.org `Review.reviewRating` in JSON-LD or microdata) exist at 9 Publications:** AllMusic, The Line of Best Fit, DIY, musicOMH, God Is In The TV, Spectrum Culture (microdata), The Independent, Evening Standard, and (for the API) The Guardian. **Pitchfork, NME, Rolling Stone, Clash, Exclaim!, Paste, Consequence, Loud and Quiet, Beats Per Minute, Mojo, The Skinny, The Arts Desk** show the score only as HTML text/CSS classes/SVG star counts — parseable, but brittle.
- **Pitchfork's numeric score is not in the server-rendered HTML at all.** The JSON-LD `Review` has no `reviewRating`; the score circle renders `0.0` server-side and is filled client-side. Pitchfork is the hardest of the "big" Publications to source mechanically, and Condé Nast's User Agreement is the most hostile to it.
- **No-score Publications:** The Quietus, Stereogum, Resident Advisor (JSON-LD `Review` present, no rating), Bandcamp Daily, The FADER, The Wire. They can only feed the Critic Score if ticket 009 decides to assign a number to unscored reviews (Metacritic does; see §5).
- **Blocked to scripted fetching (Cloudflare challenge / 403):** Uncut (everything), Under the Radar (review pages; RSS works), Slant (review pages and section feeds; main feed works), SPIN (listing), AllMusic (homepage and terms; album pages and RSS *do* work), Kerrang (feeds 403 except `feed.rss`). Metacritic and AOTY pages also 403 scripted fetches, which is moot since the map rules them out.
- **Terms of use:** where a terms page exists, most large publishers explicitly forbid automated access or extraction (Condé Nast, PMC/Rolling Stone, Guardian, Bauer/Mojo, NME Networks, Clash, musicOMH, Resident Advisor, FADER). Several small Publications have no terms page linked from their homepage at all (Quietus, Stereogum, DIY, Loud and Quiet, Sputnikmusic, The Needle Drop, No Ripcord, Northern Transmissions, Bandcamp Daily). Quotes in §3.
- **Legal gist (US):** a numeric score is a fact and facts are uncopyrightable (*Feist*); a compilation of scores gets only "thin" protection over selection/arrangement, and "a subsequent compiler remains free to use the facts". Scraping public pages is not a CFAA crime in the Ninth Circuit (*hiQ*, reinforced by *Van Buren*). The live risk is **contract**: a court found hiQ breached LinkedIn's user agreement (Nov 2022), while *Meta v. Bright Data* (Jan 2024) held logged-out scraping of public data did not breach Meta's terms, and *X v. Bright Data* (May 2024) held ToS-based claims over public data were preempted by copyright where the site was only a non-exclusive licensee. None of that is settled circuit-level law. The UK adds a *database right* and a TDM exception that is **non-commercial research only**.
- **Cleanest legal path found:** The Guardian's Content API is an actual licence (free Developer tier, 500 calls/day, non-commercial) that returns `starRating` — but its terms require deleting or re-requesting all OP Content every 24 hours, which cuts against storing history. Feeds are also an implied licence to read, but not obviously to republish a score.
- **Seed history:** Kaggle/Zenodo Pitchfork datasets (18k–24k reviews with scores, 1999–2021) are the only substantial per-Publication historical score sets found. Wikidata `P444` has scores for just 2,881 albums, 85% of them AllMusic. The "30,000 albums aggregated ratings" Kaggle set is derived from AOTY/Metacritic — using it would be scraping-by-proxy of sources the map rules out.

## 2. Method

Probed 2026-09-01/02 UTC from a residential Windows machine with a Chrome-like User-Agent, using `urllib`/`curl` (scripts in the session scratchpad, not committed). For each Publication:

1. Tried candidate feed URLs; recorded the first that returned a real `<rss>`/`<feed>` document, item count, and whether any score-like token appears in the first item.
2. Fetched one recent review page (from the feed, or a listing page), extracted every `<script type="application/ld+json">` block, and looked for `Review` / `reviewRating`, plus `itemprop="reviewRating"` microdata and score-like HTML (`8/10`, `★★★★`, `class="rating"`, star SVG counts).
3. Fetched `/robots.txt` and summarised the `User-agent: *` rules and any AI-crawler blocks.
4. Discovered the terms page from homepage footer links, fetched it, and pulled sentences mentioning scraping/crawling/robots/automation/data mining.

Where scripted fetches were blocked, I used a real browser to at least read the listing page. I did not sign up for anything. Terms quotes are the sentence as it appeared on the date above; publishers change these.

## 4. Legal shape

### 4a. US — is a score a fact?

- *Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991) (<https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/499/340>): "No one may claim originality as to facts" (at 345); "sweat of the brow" rejected because it "eschewed the most fundamental axiom of copyright law—that no one may copyright facts or ideas" (at 352); "The copyright in a factual compilation is thin" (at 349); and "A subsequent compiler remains free to use the facts contained in another's publication to aid in preparing a competing work, so long as the competing work does not feature the same selection and arrangement" (at 349).
- US Copyright Office Circular 33, *Works Not Protected by Copyright* (<https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf>): copyright excludes "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery"; protection for selection/arrangement "would be limited to the selection and arrangement of that specific content".
- Applied: a Publication's numeric score is a fact about the review. A per-Publication list of scores is a compilation; ours would be our own selection/arrangement. The review *text* is fully protected — quoting more than a short excerpt is the copyright exposure, not the score. **Uncertainty:** no case found that addresses review *scores* specifically; the inference from *Feist* is strong but not a holding.
- Precedent-by-practice: Metacritic, AOTY and Rotten Tomatoes have aggregated scores with attribution and links for two decades. That is evidence the industry tolerates it, not a legal ruling; I found no litigation over score aggregation itself.

### 4b. US — scraping public pages (CFAA and after)

- *Van Buren v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 1648 (2021): the CFAA's "exceeds authorized access" clause covers "those who obtain information from particular areas in the computer—such as files, folders, or databases—to which their computer access does not extend. It does not cover those who… have improper motives for obtaining information that is otherwise available to them." Liability "stems from a gates-up-or-down inquiry" (quoted in *hiQ* at 34–35). Cooley's summary: the Court rejected reading the statute to "incorporate[] purpose-based limits contained in contracts and workplace policies" (<https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2021/2021-06-09-us-supreme-court-computer-fraud-abuse-act-van-buren>; CRS LSB10616 at <https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10616>).
- *hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn*, No. 17-16783 (9th Cir. Apr. 18, 2022) (<https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2022/04/18/17-16783.pdf>): "the concept of 'without authorization' does not apply to public websites" (at 36); "It is likely that when a computer network generally permits public access to its data, a user's accessing that publicly available data will not constitute access without authorization under the CFAA" (at 40); a public website "has erected no gates to lift or lower in the first place" (at 36). The court expressly considered only the CFAA defence and "express[ed] no opinion" on LinkedIn's DMCA, trespass and misappropriation claims (at 20). EFF's reading (<https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2022/04/scraping-public-websites-still-isnt-crime-court-appeals-declares>) flags the unresolved trespass-to-chattels avenue.
- *hiQ v. LinkedIn* on remand, N.D. Cal. Nov. 2022 (Proskauer summary: <https://www.proskauer.com/blog/court-finds-hiq-breached-linkedins-terms-prohibiting-scraping-but-in-mixed-ruling-declines-to-grant-summary-judgment-to-either-party-as-to-certain-key-issues>): the court found hiQ breached LinkedIn's User Agreement by scraping and by using fake "turker" accounts; "a warning to members that a third party may collect their public-facing data is not a blessing for third parties to do so through expressly prohibited means." The case ended in a Dec. 2022 consent judgment with a permanent injunction and $500,000 (Privacy World: <https://www.privacyworld.blog/2022/12/linkedins-data-scraping-battle-with-hiq-labs-ends-with-proposed-judgment/>). **Net:** the CFAA is not the risk; breach of contract is, *if* you are bound by the terms.
- *Meta Platforms v. Bright Data*, N.D. Cal. Jan. 23, 2024 (Judge Chen; Farella and Goldman/McCarthy summaries: <https://www.fbm.com/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/>, <https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/01/game-on-bright-data-scores-major-victory-in-web-scraping-dispute-with-meta-guest-blog-post.htm>): Meta's terms "do not apply to and prohibit Bright Data's scraping of publicly available data while logged off"; terms bind users, and a survival clause could not bind a former user in perpetuity. Caveats from the commentators: district-court level, contract-specific, other doctrines untouched.
- *X Corp. v. Bright Data*, N.D. Cal. May 9, 2024 (Judge Alsup; Skadden and MoFo summaries: <https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/05/district-court-adopts-broad-view>, <https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240604-california-federal-court-holds-x-s-claims>): breach-of-contract and tortious-interference claims over scraping public posts were preempted by the Copyright Act because "Since X Corp. is merely a non-exclusive licensee of user-owned X content, allowing X Corp. to prohibit others from using that content would give it greater rights than it is entitled under the Copyright Act." **Distinguishing point for us:** Publications *own* their reviews (they are not mere licensees of user content), so this preemption argument is weaker against them; but a *score* is an uncopyrightable fact, which pulls the other way. Unsettled.
- **Browsewrap vs clickwrap.** All of the above turns on whether a visitor who never clicked "I agree" is bound by terms linked in a footer. Courts split; the *Meta* and *X* rulings suggest a logged-out reader of public pages often is not. Not settled law; do not rely on it.

### 4c. UK/EU (the site is likely operated from the UK)

- **Database right** — Copyright and Rights in Databases Regulations 1997, reg. 13(1): a database right subsists "if there has been a substantial investment in obtaining, verifying or presenting the contents of the database"; reg. 16(1)–(2): infringement is extracting or re-utilising "all or a substantial part of the contents", and "the repeated and systematic extraction or re-utilisation of insubstantial parts … may amount to the extraction or re-utilisation of a substantial part" (<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/3032/regulation/13>, <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/3032/regulation/16>). A Publication's review archive is arguably a database; systematically harvesting every score from it is the pattern reg. 16(2) targets. **Uncertainty:** whether a review site's *scores* count as a "substantial part" of its database, and whether the investment was in the reviews (creation, not protected) rather than the database (protected), is exactly the kind of question UK/EU courts have decided both ways.
- **Text and data mining** — CDPA 1988 s.29A permits copies "for the sole purpose of research for a non-commercial purpose" only (<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/29A>). The 2022 proposal to widen this to commercial TDM was withdrawn in 2023 (Herbert Smith Freehills: <https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/ip/2023-03/uk-withdraws-plans-for-broader-text-and-data-mining-tdm-copyright-and-database-right-exception/>). An open-source non-profit site is not obviously "research".
- **Contract** — the Guardian's ToS explicitly claim to override robots.txt; Bauer forbids screen scraping "for commercial or non-commercial purposes". No UK case found on browsewrap for scrapers; treat as exposure.

### 4d. Risk summary for the map

| Approach | Legal exposure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Consume RSS/API the Publication publishes; store score + URL + attribution + headline | Lowest. Feeds are offered for consumption; the score is a fact. PMC's terms still bar "commercial or promotional" reuse of feed content; Guardian's OP terms bar retention >24h | The Needle Drop and Guardian are the only ones where the feed/API carries the score |
| Fetch the review page linked from the feed, parse the score, store score + URL + attribution | Low-to-moderate. CFAA risk effectively nil for public pages (*hiQ*/*Van Buren*). Contract risk depends on browsewrap enforceability and the clause's breadth (Condé Nast/PMC/Guardian/Bauer/RA clauses are broad). UK database-right risk rises with volume and systematic backfill | Respect robots.txt, identify the crawler, rate-limit, cache, never republish text beyond a short excerpt |
| Backfill decades of history by crawling archives | Highest. This is "repeated and systematic extraction" (UK reg. 16(2)) and the pattern in *hiQ* | Prefer existing datasets (§6) or start from launch date |
| Republish review text or long excerpts | Copyright infringement, full stop | Keep to headline + link, or a ≤1-sentence excerpt |
| Ask Publications for permission / a feed with scores | Removes contract risk; costs time | Worth trying for the top 3–5 once the list exists (Guardian already has a commercial key path; RA's terms explicitly contemplate written agreements) |

## 5. How AOTY and Metacritic normalise

- **Metacritic** (support article "How do you compute METASCORES?", <https://metacritichelp.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/14478499933079-How-do-you-compute-METASCORES>): "For each review found, we will take the score given by the critic and convert it to a 0-100 point scale. (For those critics who do not provide a score, we'll assign a score from 0-100 based on the general impression given by the review.)" The Metascore "is a weighted average of the individual critic scores" — "we have assigned weights to each publication … thus making some publications count more". "In addition, for our movie and music sections, all of the weighted averages are normalized before generating the METASCORE … graded on a curve … Generally, higher scores are pushed higher, and lower scores are pushed lower." The per-publication weights and the grade-conversion table are not published (the "Can I see all of your grade conversion scales?" article returned 404/403).
- **AlbumOfTheYear**: the FAQ (<https://www.albumoftheyear.org/faq/>) documents nothing about the method. Observed on an AOTY publication page (Consequence, letter grades) the site converts A→100, A-→91, B+→83, B-→67, C→50, D→33 — i.e. an evenly spaced 12-step ladder with ~8.3 points per step. A star Publication presumably maps ×20 (**inferred, not documented**). AOTY's critic score appears to be an unweighted mean of converted scores; I found no statement of a minimum review count or weighting.
- **Implication for 009:** both aggregators first map every Publication onto 0–100, then average. The decisions left to us are (a) the per-format conversion table (letters, half-stars, /10 decimals, percentages), (b) whether unscored reviews get a number, (c) whether Publications are weighted, and (d) whether to curve. Metacritic does all four; AOTY appears to do only (a).
