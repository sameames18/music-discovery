# Legal and privacy obligations (ticket 006)

**This is not legal advice.** It is a research note compiled from primary sources (statute text, regulator guidance, licence text, first-party developer terms) by an agent, for a one-person, US-hosted, no-revenue, open-source site. Where a real lawyer is warranted it is flagged inline and collected in [Where a lawyer is warranted](#where-a-lawyer-is-warranted). Every quoted clause links to the source that owns it.

Vocabulary is from `CONTEXT.md`: Member, Rating, Review, Log, Library, Favourite, Import, Source, Album, Catalog, Tag, Popularity Proxy, Publication, Critic Review, Member Page.

## Answer in brief

1. **GDPR / UK GDPR.** A US site with no EU establishment is caught only if it *targets* people in the EU/UK (offers them the service, or monitors their behaviour). Mere accessibility from the EU is not enough; an English-language site with no EU-specific features is on the "not targeting" side of the EDPB's factors. But the site is public, multi-user, and will foreseeably have EU Members, so the cheap, honest position is to *behave as if* the core GDPR duties apply: a privacy notice with the Art. 13 contents, data export, deletion, one-month response, and no tracking/profiling for advertising. Doing that also keeps the site out of the Art. 3(2)(b) "monitoring" limb, which is the limb a hobbyist actually risks tripping (analytics cookies, fingerprinting). The Art. 27 EU representative requirement is the one thing to *not* do casually: it only bites if Art. 3(2) applies, and the "occasional processing" exemption is unlikely to cover a site whose whole purpose is regular processing of Member data. This is the first lawyer question.
2. **CCPA.** Does not apply. It covers *for-profit* businesses over one of three thresholds ($25M revenue; 100,000+ consumers' data bought/sold/shared; 50%+ revenue from selling PI). A no-revenue site clears none. **CalOPPA does apply** in practice (any "commercial" website collecting PII from Californians must post a privacy policy with specified contents) and California's breach-notification statute applies to any person or business conducting business in California that holds personal information. Treat the CalOPPA content list as the floor for the privacy policy.
3. **Privacy policy and ToS.** Contents are fixed by the union of GDPR Art. 13, CalOPPA §22575(b), and Spotify Developer Terms §V.12. Three open-licensed template sets exist: GitHub site-policy (CC0), Basecamp policies (CC BY), Automattic Legalmattic (CC BY-SA 4.0).
4. **Spotify.** Developer Policy §I.1.b and Developer Terms §V.8 / Appendix A §5.c: when a Member disconnects, delete their Spotify personal data (Appendix A says within five days). The product must offer an easily accessible disconnect. Spotify also demands a privacy policy with specified contents, names itself a third-party beneficiary of the site's ToS, bans AI/ML training on Spotify Content, bans transferring data to another service, and bans "integrating with streams or content from another service". Depth is ticket 003's; this note lists only what the data model and policies must carry.
5. **Section 230.** A US host is not the "publisher or speaker" of Members' Reviews, and moderating does not forfeit that. §230 does *not* cover intellectual property claims, so a Review that pastes copyrighted text (lyrics, a whole Critic Review) is a copyright exposure, not a §230-protected one. The DMCA §512(c) safe harbour covers that gap but only if the site registers a designated agent with the Copyright Office ($6, renew every three years), has a repeat-infringer policy that Members are told about, and takes material down on notice. A takedown/moderation path is therefore *required* for the copyright safe harbour and merely wise for everything else.
6. **AGPL-3.0.** Section 13 obliges anyone who *modifies* the program and runs it as a network service to offer users the Corresponding Source. As copyright holder Sam is not bound by his own licence, but the community norm and the point of choosing AGPL is to do it anyway: a visible "source code" link satisfying §13 and the §0/§5(d) Appropriate Legal Notices. MusicBrainz data is not code and does not interact with AGPL; its supplementary data (Tags, ratings) carries a *non-commercial* licence, which is a data-licence question, not a code-licence one. The Spotify Web API is a REST service with no code licence to conflict; only Spotify's proprietary SDKs (Web Playback) would be a linking question, and the product does not need them.
7. **Cover art and excerpts.** Reduced-size cover thumbnails used as an index/reference tool have two Ninth Circuit fair-use holdings behind them (*Kelly v. Arriba Soft*, *Perfect 10 v. Amazon*). Short quoted excerpts from Critic Reviews, attributed and linked, fall squarely in §107's "criticism, comment, news reporting" preamble; there is no safe word count. Full-size art and long excerpts are where the risk lives. The Cover Art Archive itself says "Use the images at your own risk."

## Findings by question

### 1. GDPR / UK GDPR exposure

**Territorial scope.** Article 3(2): "This Regulation applies to the processing of personal data of data subjects who are in the Union by a controller or processor not established in the Union, where the processing activities are related to: (a) the offering of goods or services, irrespective of whether a payment of the data subject is required, to such data subjects in the Union; or (b) the monitoring of their behaviour as far as their behaviour takes place within the Union." ([Art. 3](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-3-gdpr/))

**"Free" is not a defence.** Recital 23: the processing "should be subject to this Regulation where the processing activities are related to offering goods or services to such data subjects irrespective of whether connected to a payment." ([Recital 23](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-23/)) The EDPB: "Whether the activity of a controller or processor not established in the Union is to be considered as an offer of a good or a service is not therefore dependent whether payment is made in exchange for the goods or services provided" (Guidelines 3/2018, p. 16).

**Accessibility alone is not targeting.** Recital 23, as quoted by the EDPB (p. 17): "the mere accessibility of the controller's, processor's or an intermediary's website in the Union, of an email address or of other contact details, or the use of a language generally used in the third country where the controller is established, is insufficient to ascertain such intention, factors such as the use of a language or a currency generally used in one or more Member States with the possibility of ordering goods and services in that other language, or the mentioning of customers or users who are in the Union, may make it apparent that the controller envisages offering goods or services to data subjects in the Union." The EDPB adds (p. 15): "the provision is aimed at activities that intentionally, rather than inadvertently or incidentally, target individuals in the EU", and (p. 18) "when goods or services are inadvertently or incidentally provided to a person on the territory of the Union, the related processing of personal data would not fall within the territorial scope of the GDPR."

The EDPB's factor list (pp. 17-18), to be weighed "possibly in combination": naming the EU or a Member State; paying for search referencing or marketing aimed at an EU audience; international nature of the activity; EU phone numbers/addresses; a non-US TLD such as `.de` or `.eu`; travel instructions from Member States; mention of an international clientele "in particular by presentation of accounts written by such customers"; a language or currency of a Member State; delivery of goods in Member States.

Two of those factors deserve a note for this product. "Mention of an international clientele ... by presentation of accounts written by such customers" could be argued against a site whose public Member Pages show reviews by people who say they are in Berlin. And Example 16 (p. 19) is the closest analogue: a Swiss university's application platform open to anyone, with no EU-specific advertising and CHF-only payment, was *not* subject to GDPR; it became subject the moment it advertised specifically in German and Austrian universities. Read: a plain English-language site with no EU-directed features is on the safe side of the line, and stays there as long as nothing EU-directed is added.

**Monitoring is the limb to watch.** The EDPB (p. 20): "The EDPB does not consider that any online collection or analysis of personal data of individuals in the EU would automatically count as 'monitoring'. It will be necessary to consider the controller's purpose for processing the data and, in particular, any subsequent behavioural analysis or profiling techniques involving that data." Its examples of monitoring include "Behavioural advertisement", "Online tracking through the use of cookies or other tracking techniques such as fingerprinting", and "Market surveys and other behavioural studies based on individual profiles". A Taste Profile is, on its face, "analysing or predicting her or his personal preferences" (Recital 24 wording, p. 19), which is why S1 matters: keep the Engine's profiling to something Members explicitly opt into by using the product, and keep third-party tracking out entirely. This is a genuine grey area and is flagged for a lawyer.

**Per-activity assessment.** The EDPB (p. 14): "a controller or processor may be subject to the GDPR in relation to some of its processing activities but not subject to the GDPR in relation to other processing activities."

**Is a policy plus export/deletion enough?** If Art. 3(2) does not apply, nothing is legally required and those measures are good practice. If it does apply, the full Regulation applies, and the additional items are: an EU representative (Art. 27), a record of processing (Art. 30 — the under-250 exemption falls away when processing is "not occasional"), breach notification to a supervisory authority (Art. 33), and lawful-basis analysis for each purpose (Art. 6). No DPO is needed for a site of this kind (Art. 37 triggers on public authorities, large-scale monitoring, or special-category data at scale).

**Article 27 representative.** "Where Article 3(2) applies, the controller or the processor shall designate in writing a representative in the Union." Exemption: "processing which is occasional, does not include, on a large scale, processing of special categories of data ... and is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons". ([Art. 27](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-27-gdpr/)) The EDPB (p. 25): "a processing activity can only be considered as 'occasional' if it is not carried out regularly, and occurs outside the regular course of business or activity of the controller or processor." A site that continuously stores Member Libraries is not "occasional" processing, so the exemption is thin. The EDPB also notes (p. 24) that the representative role "can be assumed by a wide range of commercial and non-commercial entities, such as law firms, consultancies, private companies" and (p. 25) that failing to name the representative in the privacy notice is itself a transparency breach. Paid representative services exist; this note did not price them (budget rule).

**UK GDPR.** Article 3(2) UK GDPR mirrors the EU text with "the United Kingdom" substituted, and Article 27(1) reads "Where Article 3(2) applies, the controller or the processor shall designate in writing a representative in the United Kingdom", with the same occasional-processing exemption. ([UK Art. 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/3), [UK Art. 27](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/27)) legislation.gov.uk shows a pending amendment to Art. 27(4) via S.I. 2026/386; its text was not retrievable (see [Could not verify](#could-not-verify)). ICO guidance pages returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches, so the ICO's own commentary is not quoted here.

**Member rights that shape the data model.**
- Art. 17(1): erasure "without undue delay" where "the personal data are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which they were collected" or "the data subject withdraws consent". ([Art. 17](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/))
- Art. 20(1): the right to "receive the personal data concerning him or her, which he or she has provided to a controller, in a structured, commonly used and machine-readable format". ([Art. 20](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-20-gdpr/))
- Art. 12(3): respond "without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt of the request"; Art. 12(5): "free of charge". ([Art. 12](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-12-gdpr/))
- Art. 13(1)-(2): the privacy-notice content list (see M2). ([Art. 13](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-13-gdpr/))
- Art. 8(1): consent age 16 by default; "Member States may provide by law for a lower age ... provided that such lower age is not below 13 years". ([Art. 8](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-8-gdpr/))
- Art. 30(5): record-keeping exemption for under-250-person organisations "unless ... the processing is not occasional". ([Art. 30](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-30-gdpr/))
- Art. 33(1): notify the supervisory authority of a breach "not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it", unless "unlikely to result in a risk". ([Art. 33](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-33-gdpr/))

### 2. CCPA and the California laws that actually apply

**CCPA thresholds.** Cal. Civ. Code §1798.140(d)(1) defines a covered "business" as a for-profit entity doing business in California that "(A) ... had annual gross revenues in excess of twenty-five million dollars ($25,000,000) in the preceding calendar year", or "(B) ... annually buys, sells, or shares the personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households", or "(C) Derives 50 percent or more of its annual revenues from selling or sharing consumers' personal information". ([§1798.140](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.140.)) The Attorney General's page confirms "The CCPA generally does not apply to nonprofit organizations or government agencies." ([CA AG](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa)) A no-revenue site run by an individual fails the for-profit test and every threshold. **Not applicable.**

**CalOPPA (Bus. & Prof. Code §22575) does apply.** "An operator of a commercial Web site or online service that collects personally identifiable information through the Internet about individual consumers residing in California who use or visit its commercial Web site or online service shall conspicuously post its privacy policy on its Web site." §22575(b) requires the policy to: identify the categories of PII collected and the categories of third parties it may be shared with; describe any process for the consumer to review and request changes to their PII; describe how changes to the policy are notified; state its effective date; "Disclose how the operator responds to Web browser 'do not track' signals"; and disclose whether other parties may collect PII about a consumer's online activities across sites. ([§22575](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=22575.)) Whether a no-revenue hobby site is "commercial" is arguable, but the cost of complying is one web page, so treat it as applying.

**Breach notification (Cal. Civ. Code §1798.82).** Applies to "An individual or business that conducts business in California, and that owns or licenses computerized data that includes personal information". "Personal information" includes "a username or email address, in combination with a password or security question and answer that would permit access to an online account" (§1798.82(h)(2)). ([§1798.82](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.82.)) Magic-link sign-in means no password is stored, which narrows this; a leaked magic-link token table plus emails would still be an account-access credential. Every US state has a breach statute; California's is quoted because it is the one most likely to reach a site with US Members.

**COPPA.** "It is unlawful for an operator of a website or online service directed to children, or any operator that has actual knowledge that it is collecting personal information from a child, to collect personal information from a child in a manner that violates the regulations". ([15 USC §6502](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/6502)) A general-audience music site avoids "actual knowledge" by not collecting birth dates and by stating 13+ in the ToS. (FTC's COPPA FAQ returned 403; the statute is quoted instead.)

### 3. Minimal, honest privacy policy and ToS

**Privacy policy must contain** (union of GDPR Art. 13, CalOPPA §22575(b), Spotify Terms §V.12):

1. Who runs the site and how to contact them (Art. 13(1)(a); CalOPPA implicitly).
2. What is collected: email; handle/display name; Logs, Ratings, Reviews, Favourites, Reactions; Spotify-imported saved albums/tracks when a Member chooses that Source; server logs and their retention (CalOPPA (b)(1); Art. 13(1)(c); Spotify §V.12 "information collected").
3. Why, and on what basis: to run the account and the service (contract), to compute the Community Score and Recommendations (contract/legitimate interest — lawyer question), to send magic-link and account emails (contract). No marketing. (Art. 13(1)(c),(d))
4. Who receives it: the hosting provider, the transactional email provider, and — because Member Pages are public — everyone, for Ratings/Reviews/Favourites. (Art. 13(1)(e); CalOPPA (b)(1))
5. Retention: until the Member deletes the account; Spotify data deleted on disconnect within five days; server logs N days. (Art. 13(2)(a); Spotify App. A §5.c)
6. Rights: access, export, correction, deletion, restriction, objection; how to exercise them (self-service plus an email address); one-month response; right to complain to a supervisory authority. (Art. 13(2)(b)-(d); CalOPPA (b)(2))
7. Cookies: which ones (session only, first-party), and that third-party cookies are not used. (Spotify §V.12 "cookie usage and third-party cookie placement"; CalOPPA (b)(6))
8. Do Not Track statement. (CalOPPA (b)(5))
9. Transfers: data is hosted in the US. (Art. 13(1)(f))

### 5. Section 230 and Members' Reviews

**The protection.** 47 USC §230(c)(1): "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider." §230(c)(2)(A): no liability for "any action voluntarily taken in good faith to restrict access to or availability of material that the provider or user considers to be obscene, lewd, lascivious, filthy, excessively violent, harassing, or otherwise objectionable, whether or not such material is constitutionally protected". An "interactive computer service" is "any information service, system, or access software provider that provides or enables computer access by multiple users to a computer server" (§230(f)(2)), which a multi-user site is. ([47 USC §230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230))

**Who it covers.** EFF: "Section 230 allows for web operators, large and small, to moderate user speech and content as they see fit" and it has "protected us all: small blogs and websites, big platforms, and individual users". ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/issues/cda230))

**The limits.** §230(e): it does not impair federal criminal law, "any law pertaining to intellectual property", the Electronic Communications Privacy Act, or sex-trafficking claims. EFF: "It does not protect companies that violate federal criminal law. It does not protect companies that create illegal or harmful content. Nor does Section 230 protect companies from intellectual property claims."

**Consequence.** Defamation in a Review ("this producer is a thief") is the Member's problem, not the site's. Copyright in a Review (pasted lyrics, a whole Critic Review) is *not* shielded by §230; it is shielded by DMCA §512(c) only if the site has done the §512 housekeeping. Hence: takedown path is **required** for the copyright safe harbour, and moderation of everything else is a product choice §230 permits.

**DMCA §512(c) conditions.** Safe harbour if the provider "does not have actual knowledge that the material ... is infringing", "does not receive a financial benefit directly attributable to the infringing activity, in a case in which the service provider has the right and ability to control such activity", and on notice "responds expeditiously to remove, or disable access to, the material" (§512(c)(1)). It applies "only if the service provider has designated an agent to receive notifications" and the Register of Copyrights "shall maintain a current directory of agents" (§512(c)(2)). Eligibility also requires the provider to have "adopted and reasonably implemented, and informs subscribers and account holders of ... a policy that provides for the termination in appropriate circumstances of subscribers and account holders ... who are repeat infringers" (§512(i)(1)(A)). ([17 USC §512](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512)) Registration: "any designation of an agent pursuant to 17 U.S.C. 512(c)(2) must be made electronically through the Copyright Office's online registration system" and "will expire and become invalid three years after it is registered with the Office, unless the service provider renews" ([37 CFR §201.38](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/201.38)); fee "$6" ([37 CFR §201.3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/201.3)). Registering costs money ($6) and was **not** done for this ticket.

### 6. AGPL-3.0 practicalities

**Why it exists.** Preamble: "The GNU General Public License permits making a modified version and letting the public access it on a server without ever releasing its source code to the public. The GNU Affero General Public License is designed specifically to ensure that, in such cases, the modified source code becomes available to the community. It requires the operator of a network server to provide the source code of the modified version running there to the users of that server." ([AGPL-3.0](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl-3.0.html))

**Network use.** §13: "if you modify the Program, your modified version must prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network (if your version supports such interaction) an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source of your version by providing access to the Corresponding Source from a network server at no charge, through some standard or customary means of facilitating copying of software." Also §13: covered work may be linked or combined "with a work licensed under version 3 of the GNU General Public License into a single combined work". The FSF FAQ confirms: "You can always link GPLv3-covered modules with AGPLv3-covered modules, and vice versa." ([GPL FAQ, AGPLGPL](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#AGPLGPL)) The FAQ's detailed entry on what counts as "interacting remotely" could not be retrieved (see below); the §13 text itself is the operative clause.

**Does it bind Sam?** FSF FAQ: "Strictly speaking, the GPL is a license from the developer for others to use, distribute and change the program. The developer itself is not bound by it, so no matter what the developer does, this is not a 'violation' of the GPL." But "if the developer does something that would violate the GPL if done by someone else, the developer will surely lose moral standing in the community." ([GPL FAQ, DeveloperViolate](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#DeveloperViolate)) Once outside contributors' code is merged, Sam is a licensee of *their* contributions and §13 binds him for real. Practically: do M8 from day one.

**What to display.** §0 defines "Appropriate Legal Notices" as a "convenient and prominently visible feature that (1) displays an appropriate copyright notice, and (2) tells the user that there is no warranty for the work (except to the extent that warranties are provided), that licensees may convey the work under this License, and how to view a copy of this License." §5(d): "If the work has interactive user interfaces, each must display Appropriate Legal Notices". A footer with copyright line, "no warranty", a link to the AGPL text, and a link to the source at the running commit satisfies §0, §5(d), and §13 together.

**Corresponding Source.** §1: "all the source code needed to generate, install, and (for an executable work) run the object code" but "does not include the work's System Libraries, or general-purpose tools or generally available free programs which are used unmodified in performing those activities but which are not part of the work." So the database engine, runtime, and unmodified npm/pip dependencies are not part of what must be offered; the repo plus its lockfile is.

**Data is not covered.** FSF FAQ: "copyright law does not give you any say in the use of the output people make from their data using your program." ([GPL FAQ, GPLOutput](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#GPLOutput)) The AGPL governs the code; the Catalog, Tags, Ratings and Reviews carry their own terms (MusicBrainz's, and whatever the ToS grants for Member content). The map should decide separately what licence the site's own aggregate data (Critic Scores, Community Scores) is published under; that is not an AGPL question.

**Dependency conflicts.**
- *MusicBrainz data*: CC0 core / CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 supplementary. Data licences and code licences do not interact; no conflict. The NC term is a constraint on the *site's use of Tags/ratings*, not on the AGPL code.
- *Spotify Web API*: a REST service governed by the Developer Terms, not a code licence; nothing to link. If a helper client library is used, pick one under MIT/Apache-2.0/BSD (all GPL-compatible). Spotify's proprietary SDKs (Web Playback SDK, iOS/Android SDKs) are not GPL-compatible and would be a §13/§5 problem if bundled; the product does not stream and does not need them.
- *GPLv3 libraries*: compatible with AGPLv3 by §13.
- Anything under a licence the FSF lists as GPL-incompatible (some "source-available" or non-commercial licences) cannot be combined; check each dependency when ticket 017 fixes the stack.

### 7. Cover art and Critic Review excerpts

**Statute.** 17 USC §107: "the fair use of a copyrighted work ... for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching ..., scholarship, or research, is not an infringement of copyright", weighed by (1) "the purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes"; (2) "the nature of the copyrighted work"; (3) "the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole"; (4) "the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work". ([17 USC §107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)) Copyright Office: "There is no formula to ensure that a predetermined percentage or amount of a work ... may be used without permission." Courts "are more likely to find that nonprofit educational and noncommercial uses are fair" and favour "transformative" uses "that add something new, with a further purpose or different character". ([Fair use](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/))

**Thumbnails.** *Kelly v. Arriba Soft*, 336 F.3d 811 (9th Cir. 2003): reproduction of photographs "as thumbnail images was fair use. The court deemed the use transformative because the thumbnails served an entirely different function than the original images ... the search engine instead 'functions as a tool to help index and improve access to images on the internet and their related web sites.' The court also found that the lower-resolution thumbnail images did not harm the market for or value of plaintiff's images." Outcome: "Fair use found". ([Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/kelly-arriba-9thcir2003.pdf)) *Perfect 10 v. Amazon*, 508 F.3d 1146 (9th Cir. 2007): thumbnails "highly transformative" because "a search engine transformed an image into a pointer directing a user to a source of information and provided a social benefit by incorporating an original work into an electronic reference tool"; "market harm could not be presumed". ([Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/perfect10-amazon-9thcir2007.pdf)) An album Catalog showing a small cover next to an Album record is the "electronic reference tool" pattern. These are Ninth Circuit rulings; other circuits are not bound, and neither case involved a non-search site, so this is strong but not settled — lawyer if a label ever complains.

**Cover Art Archive.** "The Cover Art Archive is stored on the Internet Archive's servers and curated by the MusicBrainz community." "These covers are being collected for archival purposes, and the collection will be accessible to the public. Use the images at your own risk." ([MusicBrainz: Cover Art Archive](https://musicbrainz.org/doc/Cover_Art_Archive)) The CAA front page: "All images are copyrighted by their respective copyright owners." ([coverartarchive.org](https://coverartarchive.org/)) No licence is granted; the site's basis for display is fair use, as above. Linking to CAA's hosted image (rather than copying) does not change the analysis much after *Perfect 10* but does mean the site is not the party that stores the full-size file.

**Critic Review excerpts.** Quoting a sentence or two from a Publication's review, attributed and linked, to present its score is "criticism, comment, news reporting" in §107's own words, uses a small amount, and does not substitute for the review. Storing or displaying the full text is the opposite on factor (3) and (4). Recommended data model: `critic_review(publication, url, author, score_raw, score_normalised, pull_quote <= ~300 chars, published_at)`; no `body`. This coordinates with tickets 004/008/009.

**Lyrics.** Not fair use in any realistic reading for this product; forbid in Reviews (S8).
